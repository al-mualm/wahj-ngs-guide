From DNA to data to report

Clinical NGS from specimen to signed report

This lecture teaches the real chain behind a trustworthy sequencing result: specimen selection, extraction quality, library design, sequencing chemistry, FASTQ QC, alignment, variant calling, annotation, ACMG-style evidence review, and the final report.

Prepared by

Mahmood Al-Mualm

CEO of Wahj Al-DNA

wahj-dna.com.iq/en/home

This lecture is aligned with the Clinical WES/WGS and NGS Master Book - Second Edition and uses ACMG-style germline interpretation logic as a teaching framework.

It is educational. It does not replace validated SOPs, accredited assay performance claims, or director sign-out.

1. The controlled chain

Teach NGS as a linked process, not a single machine

A reportable NGS result is not a single output file. It is a controlled chain of biological, technical, and interpretive steps. The explorer below lets students move through the chain one stage at a time.

Start with the question, not the machine

The first decision is whether the phenotype, disease mechanism, specimen quality, and reporting need are best served by WES, WGS, a panel, RNA fusion testing, or another assay.

What happens

The team defines the intended use, required variant classes, turnaround time, and reportable scope.

What to check

Phenotype breadth, inheritance model, suspected mechanism, and whether RNA, CNV, SV, or repeat evidence is needed.

Main output

A justified test choice: WES, WGS, panel, RNA assay, or reflex pathway.

If weak

The most common failure is ordering the wrong assay and then treating a negative result as if it excluded disease.

Specimen quality decides whether the later files can be trusted

Germline and somatic workflows fail differently. Blood, saliva, FFPE tumor, bone marrow, and plasma cfDNA each carry their own contamination, degradation, and identity risks.

What happens

The laboratory checks identifiers, specimen type, collection conditions, and storage history.

What to check

Post-transplant donor DNA risk, FFPE artifact risk, bacterial DNA in saliva, tumor purity, and fixative effects.

Main output

An accepted specimen with defined clinical use and documented chain of custody.

If weak

A later BAM or VCF cannot rescue unresolved specimen identity or severe pre-analytic degradation.

Extraction QC comes before the first FASTQ file exists

Quantity, purity, and integrity determine whether the library will be complex, whether duplicates will be high, and whether certain targets may drop out.

What happens

DNA or RNA is extracted, quantified, checked for purity, and screened for fragmentation.

What to check

Qubit for concentration, 260/280 for purity, DIN for DNA integrity, RIN or DV200 for RNA quality, and contamination signals.

Main output

Nucleic acid that meets the validated input requirement for the chosen library protocol.

If weak

Low input and degraded molecules cause high duplicate rate, library failure, and uneven coverage later.

Library design controls what the bioinformatician can claim later

Clinical WES is usually hybrid-capture based. The capture kit, target BED, PCR design, and index strategy directly shape coverage breadth, GC behavior, duplicates, and the reportable region.

What happens

Fragments receive adapters and indexes, then may undergo capture or amplification depending on the assay.

What to check

Capture kit version, target BED matching the reference build, unique dual indexes when needed, and whether UMIs are validated.

Main output

A machine-ready library with documented chemistry and expected reportable target.

If weak

A WES BAM without the correct target BED cannot support clinical exon coverage claims.

Platform and read design should match the biological question

Short-read systems often use paired-end 100 bp or 150 bp designs for WES and WGS. Long-read systems are chosen when repeats, structural variants, phasing, or difficult homologous regions matter.

What happens

The library is sequenced with platform-specific chemistry and instrument controls.

What to check

Read length, single-end vs paired-end configuration, lane balance, throughput, and batch design.

Main output

Base calls and qualities that become FASTQ files, plus run metrics such as Q30 and yield.

If weak

Longer reads do not fix poor capture design, pseudogene problems, or an assay that lacks the required depth.

FASTQ QC tells you whether the raw data can support the downstream analysis

It does not diagnose the patient. It tells you whether the run produced usable reads or whether adapter signal, severe quality decay, or duplication should trigger trimming or investigation.

What happens

Per-base quality, adapter content, GC pattern, duplication, and read length distribution are reviewed.

What to check

Q30, late-cycle quality collapse, abnormal GC pattern, severe adapter rise, and unexpectedly high duplication.

Main output

Raw reads that are accepted as-is, trimmed, or stopped for root-cause analysis.

If weak

Never trim just to make a QC plot look prettier while hiding a failed library.

Alignment turns reads into genomic evidence

BAM or CRAM QC is where reference policy, mapping behavior, insert size, duplicate burden, contamination, and callable regions become visible.

What happens

Reads are aligned to a locked reference build, sorted, indexed, and summarized.

What to check

Mapping rate, proper pairing, insert size, duplicate rate, contamination, and local coverage at clinically relevant loci.

Main output

Analysis-ready BAM/CRAM plus coverage and QC metrics that show whether the sample is callable.

If weak

Do not mix builds casually. Reference, transcript set, target BED, and annotation resources must agree.

VCF generation and annotation produce candidates, not final truth

Variant callers, filters, annotation versions, and prioritization logic should all be documented. The final candidate list is only the start of evidence review.

What happens

SNVs, indels, CNVs, SVs, or expression results are called and annotated with transcript and database context.

What to check

Plausible variant counts, database versioning, transcript policy, and whether filtering logic is recorded.

Main output

A candidate set that is ready for phenotype correlation and evidence-based classification.

If weak

Filtering is not classification. A candidate should not be upgraded before evidence review.

Interpretation and reporting are controlled human gates

The final report should be readable, traceable, and defensible. It must not hide limitations behind software output, and it must not overstate VUS or out-of-scope findings.

What happens

Evidence is reviewed, a classification or tier is assigned, and the result is written in clinical language.

What to check

Phenotype fit, inheritance, evidence codes, assay scope, callable gaps, and whether confirmation is required.

Main output

A signed report with variant table, QC summary, interpretation, recommendations, and stated limitations.

If weak

A VUS is not a diagnosis, and mean depth alone cannot replace local gene or exon coverage review.

2. Pre-analytic control

Specimen acceptance, extraction, and nucleic-acid quality

Most bad sequencing reports begin before the first read exists. Students need to recognize which specimen failures later become uneven coverage, false positives, duplicate inflation, or uninterpretable regions.

PB

Peripheral blood

Usually preferred for routine germline DNA.

Watch for: post-transplant donor DNA, hematologic contamination, low volume.

SB

Saliva / buccal

Useful when blood is not available.

Watch for: bacterial DNA, food contamination, low human DNA fraction.

FF

FFPE tumor

Common in solid tumor workflows.

Watch for: fragmentation, deamination artifacts, low tumor purity.

CF

Plasma cfDNA

Useful for liquid biopsy contexts.

Watch for: low allele fraction, molecule scarcity, and ctDNA-specific library needs.

Concentration

Use Qubit for input mass before library preparation.

Low input often means high duplicates later

Purity

Check 260/280 and other contamination indicators.

Protein, phenol, or RNA carryover can distort library behavior

Integrity

DIN for DNA and RIN or DV200 for RNA show fragmentation burden.

Degraded input drives dropout and uneven coverage

Identity

Use two identifiers and verify trio or tumor-normal relationships before interpretation.

Identity errors are stop rules, not minor warnings
3. Wet-lab design

Library preparation, read design, and sequencing platforms

This is the stage where biology is converted into machine-readable molecules. It is also where many later coverage and duplication problems are created.

IL

Illumina short reads

Sequencing-by-synthesis, commonly PE100 or PE150 in WES and WGS.

  • Strong ecosystem and high routine accuracy
  • Short reads can struggle with repeats and large SVs
MG

MGI / DNBSEQ

Short-read platform using DNA nanoballs and cPAS chemistry.

  • Still follows the same FASTQ-to-BAM-to-VCF logic later
  • Validate data-conversion, quality behavior, and capture compatibility
ON

Oxford Nanopore

Long reads are useful for repeats, structural variation, and rapid flexible workflows.

  • Helps when read length matters more than classical short-read density
  • Error profiles differ from short-read systems
PB

PacBio HiFi

Long-read sequencing with strong consensus accuracy in many workflows.

  • Useful for phasing, complex regions, and some difficult variant classes
  • Different throughput and cost trade-offs from short-read platforms
Thermo Fisher workflow

Ion Torrent from AmpliSeq library to Ion Reporter result

This workshop is built around a Thermo Fisher Ion Torrent environment, so students should see how the general NGS chain maps onto Ion AmpliSeq, Ion Chef, Ion GeneStudio S5, Torrent Suite, and Ion Reporter.

Semiconductor principle

Ion Torrent chemistry converts nucleotide incorporation into an electronic signal on a semiconductor chip. The practical teaching message is simple: signal behavior, chip loading, templating, and local sequence context all matter before interpretation.

Ion Torrent QC vocabulary

Teach loading, usable reads, read length, mapped reads, on-target rate, uniformity, mean depth, amplicon dropout, polyclonal reads, low-quality reads, and no-call regions as connected signals, not isolated numbers.

Homopolymer caution

Repeated bases can make small indel interpretation more delicate. The report should not hide low local confidence behind a strong global run summary.

5. Bioinformatics chain

FASTQ, BAM/CRAM, VCF, and annotation from a student’s point of view

These file types mark the transition from raw sequencing signal to biological interpretation. Students should understand what each file means before trying to interpret the final result.

FASTQ is where the sequencer becomes data

Every record carries a read sequence and its base-quality string. FASTQ QC asks whether the run can support later alignment and calling. It does not diagnose the patient.

What it stores

Read names, nucleotide strings, plus quality strings that encode base-call confidence.

Student checks

Per-base quality, Q30, adapter rise, duplication estimate, GC pattern, and read count.

Important caution

Trimming is a corrective step, not a way to hide a failed run.

@READ_0001
ACTGCTAGATCGATCGTAGCTA
+
FFFFFFFFFFFFFIIIIIIIII

BAM or CRAM turns reads into mapped genomic evidence

Alignment tells you where each read fits relative to the locked reference build. BAM QC is where coverage, duplication, contamination, and local callability become visible.

What it stores

Reference coordinates, mapping flags, base qualities, CIGAR strings, and read groups.

Student checks

Mapping rate, proper pairs, insert size, duplicate burden, contamination, and local depth.

Important caution

The reference build, target BED, and transcript policy must match the validated pipeline.

READ_0001  99  chr7  140453136  60
76M  =  140453295  235
NM:i:0  MD:Z:76

VCF is the candidate list, not the final diagnosis

A VCF contains called variants and technical fields. It is useful only after QC, annotation, phenotype matching, and evidence review.

What it stores

Chromosome, position, REF/ALT, genotype, depth, allele balance, and caller-level annotations.

Student checks

Plausible variant counts, genotype depth, quality filters, allele balance, and family consistency.

Important caution

Filtering narrows candidates; it does not replace ACMG evidence review or tumor tiering logic.

chr7  140453136  .  A  T  742.3  PASS
DP=132;AF=0.48;MQ=60
GT:AD:DP  0/1:68,64:132

Annotation adds biological and reporting context

Annotation connects a variant to genes, transcripts, protein consequences, population frequency, ClinVar history, and other evidence families used during classification.

What it adds

Gene, transcript, exon, protein effect, disease associations, and database references.

Student checks

Transcript policy, annotation version, database date, and build consistency.

Important caution

A clinical annotation layer should be versioned so that the result is reproducible later.

GENE=CFTR
TRANSCRIPT=NM_000492.4
HGVS.c=c.1521_1523delCTT
HGVS.p=p.Phe508del
CLINVAR=Pathogenic

FASTQ

Q30, read count, GC pattern, adapter signal, duplication estimate.

Alignment

Reference build, read groups, mapping rate, proper pairing.

Coverage

Mean depth, percent ≥20x, gene or exon gaps, uniformity.

Calling

Plausible variant counts, genotype quality, allele balance, Ti/Tv sanity checks.

Annotation

VEP or ANNOVAR version, transcript set, ClinVar date, gnomAD version.

Release

Failed regions, LoD, confirmation policy, and report wording all must be clear.

Variant types workshop atlas

High-yield variant types, diseases, and reporting traps

Do not try to memorize every famous mutation in a 45-minute lecture. Use a representative atlas: one visual example, one disease link, one clinical use, and one limitation for each major variant class.

SNVs and small indels are the most familiar, but not always simple

EGFR p.Leu858Arg

Classic NSCLC predictive hotspot; report with VAF, coverage, tumor context, and therapy relevance.

KRAS p.Gly12Cys

Actionable in selected NSCLC contexts and important in colorectal cancer resistance interpretation.

BRAF p.Val600Glu

Seen in melanoma, thyroid cancer, colorectal cancer, and others; actionability depends on tumor type.

CFTR p.Phe508del

Three-base deletion causing cystic fibrosis; a good example that not every deletion is a frameshift.

BRCA1 frameshift

Germline loss-of-function example tied to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer risk.

FLT3-ITD

AML internal tandem duplication; a reminder that some indels need special detection/reporting logic.

Splice variants need transcript-level thinking

MLH1 / MSH2 splice-site

Can support Lynch syndrome when variant evidence and phenotype/family history fit.

BRCA1 canonical splice

Often treated as loss-of-function evidence when the affected transcript and mechanism are validated.

Deep intronic splice

May be missed by focused panels if outside reportable intronic boundaries.

RNA rescue

RNA evidence can clarify whether a DNA variant truly disrupts splicing.

CNVs are dosage calls, not ordinary SNVs

ERBB2 amplification

Breast and gastric cancer actionability example; orthogonal confirmation may depend on assay policy.

MYCN amplification

Neuroblastoma risk marker; shows why copy number can be prognostic.

DMD exon deletion

Duchenne/Becker example; exon-level resolution and reading frame matter.

PMP22 duplication

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1A; a clean germline dosage example.

Fusions are structure and expression events

BCR::ABL1

CML-defining fusion and a strong example of diagnostic plus treatment relevance.

EML4::ALK

NSCLC predictive fusion; RNA-based assays often help because expressed fusion evidence matters.

NTRK fusion

Tumor-agnostic actionability example, but detection and fusion partner interpretation still matter.

PML::RARA

APL-defining fusion where rapid recognition changes urgent management.

MSI and TMB are signatures, not single-gene variants

MSI-high

Important in colorectal, endometrial, and other cancers; may suggest mismatch repair deficiency.

TMB-high

Can support immunotherapy discussion in selected settings, but threshold and assay validation matter.

Negative MSI/TMB

Does not exclude all immune-response biology; report method and limitations clearly.

Panel size effect

Small panels may be less robust for broad signature estimation than validated large panels.

6. Interpretation

From candidate variants to ACMG-style evidence review

This stage is where many students over-trust software output. The workflow should be taught in the correct order: QC, alignment, coverage, calling, annotation, filtering, phenotype matching, evidence review, then classification.

80k to 5M sites

Raw variant universe before quality, relevance, and evidence filtering.

High quality + callable

Remove poor-quality or technically unsupported sites first.

Rare or clinically relevant

Frequency, tumor relevance, or assay purpose reduce the candidate set.

Consequence + gene fit

Keep candidates whose variant effect and gene biology match the use case.

Inheritance + phenotype match

Family structure, zygosity, segregation, and phenotype sharpen the shortlist.

Evidence-reviewed result

The final candidate is classified only after evidence review. Filtering alone is never classification.

Filtering is not classification

A variant can survive filtering and still remain a VUS or non-reportable. Filtering only selects candidates. Germline classification follows an evidence framework such as ACMG/AMP. Somatic reports use tiered clinical significance, not germline pathogenicity categories.

The five-tier germline framing here follows ACMG/AMP-style interpretation as summarized in your master book and reflected in the Genomatics ACMG Variant Classification Guide.

Download ACMG guideline paper (PDF)

Pathogenic

This class means the evidence is sufficient to support disease causality in the correct phenotype and inheritance context.

When it is used

When validated evidence strongly supports the variant as disease-causing in the relevant mechanism.

Clinical handling

May support diagnosis, counseling, segregation planning, or a defined clinical action.

Evidence families may include:
Population rarity
Functional evidence
Segregation / de novo data
Strong literature and database support

Likely pathogenic

Strong evidence supports causality, but the evidence is not absolute. The wording should reflect that remaining uncertainty honestly.

When it is used

When the evidence is persuasive but does not meet the full standard for pathogenic.

Clinical handling

Often actionable in the right context, but the report should still align the wording with the evidence strength.

Do not inflate certainty.
Keep phenotype and inheritance in view.
State if confirmation or family testing is still needed.

Variant of uncertain significance (VUS)

Evidence is insufficient or conflicting. A VUS is not a diagnosis and should not be used alone to direct irreversible management or predictive testing of relatives.

What it means

The variant remains a candidate of interest, but causality is not established.

How it should be handled

Keep the wording explicitly uncertain. Recommend phenotype refinement, segregation, or orthogonal evidence if appropriate.

Never teach students:
"VUS explains the disease"

Safer wording:
"Clinical significance is uncertain"

Teaching point: one of the most important lessons in NGS is learning not to overstate uncertain evidence.

Likely benign

Evidence trends against disease causality, but the supporting record is not as complete as a fully benign classification.

What it means

The variant is usually not clinically actionable as a causal finding.

What to teach

Students should understand that “not likely causal” is different from “artifact”.

Evidence may include:
Population frequency too high
Weak phenotype fit
Contradictory disease mechanism

Benign

Strong evidence argues against disease causality. It should not be reported as a causal explanation.

What it means

The evidence clearly supports that the variant is not the explanation for the disease mechanism under review.

What to teach

Benign is a classification outcome, not a comment on whether the call was technically real.

Analytical truth and clinical truth differ.
A technically real variant can still be benign.

7. Final output

What the final NGS report should contain

A final report is a controlled medical or scientific document. It should state what was tested, how well it performed, what was found, how the finding was classified, and what the key limitations are.

Negative does not mean impossible

Negative reports still need scope, coverage, LoD, and assay blind spots stated clearly.

Coverage gaps must be visible

If a clinically relevant gene or exon was not callable, the report should say so rather than hide it under a global mean depth value.

VUS wording must stay uncertain

Use cautious language such as “clinical significance is uncertain” rather than diagnosis language.

Versioning protects reproducibility

Reference build, capture kit, pipeline version, annotation database versions, and transcript policy should be recorded.

Somatic is not germline

Somatic cancer reports use AMP/ASCO/CAP-style tiering logic. Do not teach students to force tumor variants into germline ACMG pathogenicity language.

Best teaching summary for the end of the lecture

The final result is never just “a variant found by software.” It is a controlled answer built from specimen quality, validated laboratory design, sequencing performance, bioinformatics QC, local coverage review, evidence-based interpretation, and disciplined report wording.

Report-reading exercise

Read reports together like a clinical team

Use public de-identified examples as references, then teach from shorter mock reports that fit the workshop time. The goal is not to read every word. The goal is to find the result, evidence strength, actionability, limitation, and next step quickly.

Troubleshooting NGS failures

Find the failed stage before blaming the variant caller

Troubleshooting works best when students move backward through the chain: report problem, variant evidence, alignment, run metrics, library behavior, extraction QC, and finally the original specimen.

Five-minute troubleshooting script

Ask: Was the specimen acceptable? Was extraction adequate? Did the library quantify correctly? Did templating/chip loading pass? Did the relevant locus reach validated coverage? Did the caller and annotation use the correct build? Does the report wording match the evidence?

Prevention checklist

Use two identifiers, verify specimen type, document tumor content, quantify with the correct method, balance barcodes, review amplicon-level coverage, version every database, and never hide no-call regions.

Sanger confirmation and validation

Sanger is useful, but it is not a universal truth machine

Sanger sequencing is excellent for selected sequence-level confirmation and family testing, but it is weak for low-VAF somatic variants, mosaicism, CNVs, fusions, large indels, and complex mixed samples.

Heterozygous sequence variant

A clean double peak at the expected position can support confirmation of a germline SNV or simple indel when the region amplifies well.

Homozygous or hemizygous sequence variant

A single clean alternate peak can confirm a simple variant, but zygosity still depends on sample identity, locus context, and assay design.

Noisy trace or low allele fraction

Sanger usually cannot confidently confirm low-VAF tumor variants. Use a validated orthogonal method that matches the biological question.

Practical FASTQ QC

Analyze NGS FASTQ files directly in the browser

After sequencing, FASTQ files should be checked before alignment or downstream interpretation. The FASTQ QC Analyzer reviews read counts, read lengths, GC content, N content, Q20/Q30 bases, per-cycle quality, base composition, adapter-like sequences, and overrepresented reads without uploading files.

Upload FASTQ
