Clinical NGS • Lecture 1

Bioinformatics, ACMG and the command line

This lecture is designed for medical doctors who need to understand how raw sequencing data become clinically interpretable evidence. The focus is not on coding alone; it is on what must happen correctly before any result deserves to be trusted in a report.

Prepared by

Mahmood Al-Mualm

CEO of Wahj Al-DNA

Sequencers generate data, not diagnoses. Bioinformatics organizes the data. Classification organizes the evidence. Clinical interpretation still requires the right phenotype, inheritance model, test scope, and laboratory context.

Opening question

The sequencer does not produce a diagnosis

Clinical NGS starts with millions of reads, narrows to thousands of candidate variants, then to a few reportable findings. This lecture explains what removes false candidates and what converts the remaining findings into defensible classifications.

Part 1 • Clinical view

What a medical doctor should ask before trusting an NGS result

Most downstream interpretation errors begin upstream. If the sample, coverage, alignment, or phenotype context is weak, no evidence code can rescue the result.

Was the specimen suitable?

Tissue type, DNA integrity, contamination risk, and labeling errors all change the reliability of the final report.

Was the target region adequately covered?

A negative result is only meaningful if the clinically relevant exons or hotspots were actually sequenced to sufficient depth and quality.

Is the detected variant technically real?

Depth, mapping quality, base quality, allele fraction, strand bias, and local sequence context all influence whether a call is trustworthy.

Does the interpretation framework match the case?

Germline pathogenicity, somatic clinical significance, and tumor oncogenicity are different questions. They do not use interchangeable language.

Clinical rule: the report is the final checkpoint, not the first place where quality problems should be noticed.
Part 2 • What is bioinformatics?

Bioinformatics turns biological signal into computable clinical evidence

In clinical genomics, bioinformatics is not only software use. It combines biology, computing, statistics, data management, and laboratory context to produce auditable evidence from sequencing data.

Primary analysis

Signal processing and base calling convert the instrument output into sequence data.

  • BCL or raw machine signal
  • Demultiplexing
  • FASTQ generation

Secondary analysis

Reads are cleaned, aligned, and converted into technical variant calls.

  • FASTQ QC and trimming
  • Alignment to reference genome
  • Variant calling, BAM/CRAM, VCF

Tertiary analysis

Variants are annotated, filtered, prioritized, classified, and reported.

  • Population and transcript annotation
  • Phenotype-driven prioritization
  • ACMG/AMP evidence review and report drafting

Reference build and transcript choice

A genomic coordinate is meaningful only if the reference build and transcript are explicit. The same variant may receive different cDNA or protein consequences depending on transcript selection.

  • GRCh37 and GRCh38 coordinates are not interchangeable.
  • Transcript choice changes exon numbering, HGVS, and sometimes consequence class.
  • Every report should document the build and transcript used.

Variant types the pipeline may or may not detect well

Clinical NGS is not one method for one variant type. Detection performance differs between SNVs, indels, CNVs, structural variants, repeat expansions, and mtDNA heteroplasmy.

  • SNVs and small indels are the classic strengths of short-read pipelines.
  • CNV and exon-dropout logic need separate evidence and coverage review.
  • Negative results must be interpreted in the context of assay scope.

Validation, provenance, and security

A clinically defensible result requires pipeline validation, software version control, sample identity checks, audit trails, and secure handling of genomic data.

  • Sample swaps and contamination can invalidate interpretation before ACMG review starts.
  • Software version drift can change the VCF even when the patient is the same.
  • Audit trails are a laboratory requirement, not optional informatics decoration.

Part 4 • Professional roles

Who writes guidance, who accredits, who curates, and who regulates

No single organization controls genetic testing worldwide. Guidance, accreditation, evidence curation, public archives, and government regulation are different layers of responsibility.

Critical distinction: ClinVar is a public archive of submissions. ClinGen provides expert curation and criteria guidance. CAP focuses on laboratory quality systems and inspection, not just variant classification.
Part 5 • Before and after standardization

Why the 2015 ACMG/AMP paper changed how laboratories talk about variants

Before standardization, similar evidence could receive labels such as “mutation,” “polymorphism,” “deleterious,” or “unclassified” in different laboratories. The 2015 consensus paper standardized the language and weighting framework for germline Mendelian interpretation.

Before 2015

Inconsistent terminology

Terms such as mutation, disease-causing, polymorphism, deleterious, and neutral were used inconsistently and could imply stronger certainty than the evidence actually supported.

2013

Workgroup and surveys

A workgroup with ACMG, AMP, and CAP representation compared laboratory practice, surveyed multiple labs, and tested criteria and terminology against real use.

March 2015

Joint ACMG/AMP recommendation

The final paper established five germline classes, standardized evidence strengths, and published the combination rules that many laboratories still teach today.

2017 onward

Somatic frameworks separate

Later frameworks for cancer variants addressed somatic clinical significance and oncogenicity separately from germline pathogenicity.

ClinGen era

Criteria refinement

ClinGen expert panels and current guidance refine how the original criteria are applied, including PVS1 logic, PM2 strength, segregation, computation, and splicing evidence.

Part 6 • Framework separation

Germline pathogenicity, somatic clinical significance, and oncogenicity are not the same question

This distinction is essential for medical doctors. A result can be biologically important in cancer yet not use ACMG/AMP germline wording, and a germline pathogenic variant can have implications for relatives in a way that somatic findings do not.

Question Typical setting Framework Typical output language Why this matters clinically
Does this variant cause an inherited disorder? Germline Mendelian testing ACMG/AMP five-tier classes Benign to pathogenic Impacts diagnosis, counseling, recurrence risk, and relatives.
Does this tumor alteration affect diagnosis, prognosis, or therapy? Somatic cancer testing AMP/ASCO/CAP clinical significance tiers Tier I to IV significance Addresses treatment actionability and cancer management, not inherited causation.
Does this alteration contribute to cancer biology? Somatic oncogenicity review ClinGen/CGC/VICC-type oncogenicity logic Oncogenic, likely oncogenic, uncertain, etc. Biologic cancer role is not the same as immediate clinical actionability.
Part 7 • Five-tier system

ACMG/AMP classes are final conclusions, not individual evidence codes

One of the most important teaching points is that codes such as PVS1, PM2, or BS3 are pieces of evidence. The final classification is a separate judgment after those pieces are combined.

Part 11 • Interactive command line

Why the command line belongs in a clinical NGS lecture

Doctors do not need to become bioinformatics engineers, but they should understand why command-line work remains central to validation, reproducibility, provenance, and the exact recovery of a result during review or audit.

Terminal, shell, CLI

The terminal is the window. The shell interprets commands. The command-line interface is the text-based way we tell programs what to do.

Paths and files

Doctors should recognize working directory, absolute path, relative path, compressed files, and why file names must match the sample and report.

Safety and audit

Good command-line practice means using --help, documenting versions, logging commands, and avoiding destructive actions when reviewing patient data.

Part 12 • Reporting and reclassification

What the final report should say clearly, and what it should not overclaim

The last interpretive job is communication. Reports should separate detected findings, limitations, VUS language, phenotype correlation, and future reclassification pathways.

VUS communication

A VUS should remain uncertain in the report. It should not be described as disease-causing only because it is rare or located in an interesting gene.

Limitations statement

The report should state uncovered regions, low-confidence regions, assay scope, and what variant classes may not be well assessed by the method used.

ClinVar and reanalysis

ClinVar is a useful public archive, but not the only authority. Reanalysis may change a VUS or even refine a previously reported pathogenic interpretation.

Family and phenotype context

Segregation studies, updated phenotype information, copy-number testing, and orthogonal confirmation can all change how a variant is finally interpreted.

Reporting rule: a clinically good report does not only say what was found; it also says what was not confidently ruled out.
Part 13 • References and take-home points

Keep the clinical logic transparent

A good NGS lecture for doctors should end with a question: if the classification changed tomorrow, what new evidence would have caused that change?

Take-home messages

  • Sequencing data are not a diagnosis.
  • FASTQ, BAM/CRAM, and VCF are different checkpoints, not interchangeable files.
  • Detection, prioritization, classification, and reporting are separate jobs.
  • ACMG/AMP codes are evidence items; the five-tier class is the conclusion.
  • ClinGen guidance refines many original criteria and must be checked.
  • Command-line work makes the analysis reproducible, reviewable, and auditable.
